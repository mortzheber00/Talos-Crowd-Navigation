# Evaluation — Crowd Navigation for Talos

The project was built engineering-first: make the humanoid walk through a crowd.
This document retrofits the scientific frame — what questions the finished system
can actually answer, how each is measured, and what the answers were.

Numbers marked `[TBD]` are filled from the sweep output; every figure is
regenerated by `experiments/analyze.py` from the CSVs in `experiments/results/`.

---

## 1. The framing

The system is a **timescale and representation bridge**. ORCA is a 2D holonomic
velocity planner that assumes agents change velocity instantly. A biped is
neither holonomic nor instantaneous: it moves in 0.8 s footsteps and is
dynamically committed one to two steps ahead. Everything interesting about this
project lives at that seam.

So the evaluation is not "does ORCA work" (it does, it is a published algorithm)
but **what does putting ORCA on top of a biped cost, and where does the coupling
break?**

That question splits into six:

| | Question | Type |
|---|---|---|
| **Q-A** | Does each layer meet its real-time budget? | measurement |
| **Q-B** | Does the footstep bridge actually execute the velocity command? | mechanism |
| **Q-B2** | Should ORCA plan at a speed the walker can deliver? | intervention |
| **Q-C** | Does the system navigate safely across scenarios? | validation |
| **Q-D** | How much of the avoidance should the robot take on itself? | trade-off |
| **Q-E** | At what crowd density does it break? | limits |
| **Q-F** | What does onboard perception cost versus ground truth? | ablation |
| **Q-G** | Is the robot avoiding the crowd, or the crowd avoiding the robot? | attribution |

Q-C is the project's stated success criteria. Q-A, Q-B, Q-D–G are what make the
result a study rather than a demo.

**Q-G was added after Q-C came back at 100 % everywhere.** A result that good,
that uniform, is a signal to check the setup rather than to celebrate: the
pedestrians avoid the robot reciprocally, using the simulator's exact robot
state, so a crowd that parts politely around a fencepost would score identically.
Q-G removes the crowd's knowledge of the robot and re-measures. It is the
question that decides whether any of the other numbers describe the robot.

## 2. Method

### Setup

MuJoCo, Talos humanoid, 300 Hz sim/WBC, 10 Hz LIP-MPC, 1.25 Hz footstep
planner, ~30 Hz ORCA. Pedestrians are kinematic ORCA disks (r = 0.3 m,
v_max = 1.3 m/s) that reciprocally avoid each other and the robot. The robot
navigates from the origin to (4, 0).

Runs are headless (`render:=false`) and fully parameterised over ROS 2
parameters, so a sweep needs no code edits. `experiments/run_sweep.py` launches
them 10 at a time, each in its **own `ROS_DOMAIN_ID`** — without that isolation
concurrent runs share a DDS domain and one run's ORCA node steers on another
run's odometry.

### Independent variables

`scenario`, `seed`, `n_peds`, `robot_responsibility`, `safety_margin`,
`desired_speed`, `pedestrian_sensor_data`.

### Metrics (`src/bullet_sims/bullet_sims/eval_metrics.py`)

| Metric | Definition |
|---|---|
| **success** | reached goal (within 0.3 m) **and** never fell (CoM z > 0.70 m) |
| **collision-free success** | success **and** ground-truth surface gap never < 0 |
| **min clearance** | smallest robot–pedestrian *surface* gap over the run [m] |
| **path ratio** | path length / straight-line distance (1.0 = no detour) |
| **time to goal** | first time inside the goal tolerance [s] |
| **ZMP RMSE** | commanded vs measured ZMP, healthy samples only [m] |
| **speed / lateral gain** | achieved ÷ commanded velocity, smoothed over one step |
| **per-layer p95** | wall-clock cost per invocation vs that layer's period |

Two decisions worth stating explicitly, because they change what the numbers mean:

1. **Clearance is always measured on ground truth**, never on what the robot
   perceived. In perception mode a pedestrian the camera never detects would
   otherwise silently vanish from the safety statistics — the exact failure the
   experiment is meant to expose.
2. **Samples after a fall are excluded** from the collision count. A collapsed
   robot drifting into a pedestrian is a consequence of falling, not an
   avoidance failure, and double-counting it would inflate the collision rate
   in precisely the conditions where the robot already failed.

### Statistics

10 seeds per condition. The seed drives crowd layout and the preferred-velocity
noise; the simulator is otherwise deterministic (verified: the `empty` scenario
is bit-identical across seeds, so it is run 3× as a determinism check rather
than 10× as a statistic).

Proportions carry **Wilson 95 % intervals** — at n = 10 with rates near 1.0 the
textbook normal interval runs past 100 %. Paired comparisons (Q-F) use the
**Wilcoxon signed-rank test**: the pairing is by (scenario, seed), n is small,
and normality is not assumed.

## 3. Results

### Q-A — Real-time budget  → `figures/qa_timing_budget.png`

Each layer is measured against **its own period**, not a single control tick:
ORCA at 30 Hz has 33 ms, the MPC at 10 Hz has 100 ms, TSID at 300 Hz has 3.3 ms.

Measured by `qa_solo` — 4 runs, **one at a time on an otherwise idle machine**.
The sweep's own timings (10 concurrent simulations) are roughly 2× inflated and
are deliberately not quoted; a real-time claim measured under self-inflicted load
is worthless.

| layer | rate | budget | mean [ms] | p95 [ms] | % of budget |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ORCA solve | 30 Hz | 33.3 ms | 0.30 | 0.62 | **1.8 %** |
| Footstep planner | 1.25 Hz | 800 ms | 0.29 | 0.54 | 0.1 % |
| LIP-MPC (Drake QP) | 10 Hz | 100 ms | 6.04 | 11.96 | 12.0 % |
| TSID whole-body | 300 Hz | 3.33 ms | 1.30 | 2.33 | 69.8 % |
| MuJoCo step | 300 Hz | 3.33 ms | 0.70 | 1.17 | 35.0 % |
| Depth perception | 30 Hz | 33.3 ms | 30.95 | 44.74 | **134 %** |

**The project's "≤50 ms per planning step" criterion is met comfortably**: the
whole navigation stack — ORCA plus footstep planner plus MPC — costs ≈13 ms p95.

Two honest caveats:

* **The 300 Hz loop is marginally over budget in sum.** TSID (2.33 ms) plus the
  MuJoCo step (1.17 ms) is 3.5 ms p95 against a 3.33 ms period. That is why the
  simulation runs ~3× slower than realtime, and it is the tightest margin in the
  stack — not the planner.
* **Perception's 1115 ms maximum is a first-call artefact** (renderer allocation
  on the first frame), not a recurring stall. The meaningful figure is the
  44.7 ms p95, which is genuinely over its 33 ms budget.

The headline: *the avoidance planner is free.* ORCA is 1.8 % of its budget and
the footstep planner 0.1 % of its own. Everything expensive in this system is
either the whole-body controller or the perception front-end — the interesting
engineering was never the solver, and Q-G shows the solver was not what produced
the results either.

### Q-B — Does the bridge execute the command?  → `figures/qb_command_tracking.png`

The mechanism result, and the most informative thing in the study.

ORCA is configured to ask for 1.2 m/s. The reactive footstep planner clamps a
step to `1.4 · step_size_x = 0.35 m` per `step_dur = 0.8 s`, i.e. **0.44 m/s**.
So the command saturates the clamp essentially always.

Measured over 10 runs:

| quantity | value | reading |
|---|---|---|
| forward speed gain | **0.50** | half the commanded speed is delivered |
| lateral (avoidance) gain | **1.08** | the avoidance component *is* executed |
| lateral lag | **1.57 s ≈ 2 steps** | and it arrives two steps late |

This is not a tracking bug, it is a **specification mismatch**: ORCA is asking
for a speed that does not exist on this robot.

A caveat on the mechanism, because the obvious story is wrong. `_advance`
scales the *whole* displacement vector when it exceeds `max_step_len`, so one
would expect the lateral component to be attenuated in the same proportion as
the forward one — and it is not: lateral tracking is near unity while forward
tracking is halved. What saturates is the forward component, which carries
almost all of the command's magnitude; the lateral part is small enough to
survive the clamp. **So the robot does execute avoidance — just two steps late.**
The two-step lag is the irreducible cost of a planner committed one step ahead
plus one step of execution. The speed deficit is not irreducible, and Q-B2 fixes
it.

### Q-B2 — Matching ORCA's speed to the walker  → `figures/qb2_speed_matching.png`

The intervention Q-B implies: sweep `desired_speed` ∈ {0.3, 0.45, 0.6, 1.2} m/s.

Prediction: lowering it to ~0.45 m/s should **improve command tracking at no cost
in time-to-goal**, because the extra commanded speed was never delivered.

| `desired_speed` [m/s] | achieved [m/s] | speed gain | t_goal [s] | path ratio | collision-free |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.30 | 0.299 | 1.02 | 14.53 | 1.075 | 100 % |
| 0.45 | 0.387 | 0.93 | 10.73 | 1.024 | 100 % |
| **0.60** | **0.403** | **0.76** | **10.18** | **1.016** | 100 % |
| 1.20 (shipped) | 0.408 | 0.45 | 10.28 | 1.048 | 100 % |

**Confirmed, with a sharper answer than predicted.** Achieved speed saturates at
≈0.40 m/s — close to the 0.44 m/s the footstep clamp allows — and does not move
once `desired_speed` passes 0.6. Between 0.6 and 1.2 the robot asks for twice the
speed and arrives *no sooner* (10.18 s vs 10.28 s), while the command's gain
falls from 0.76 to 0.45.

The recommendation is **`desired_speed ≈ 0.6 m/s`**: fastest time-to-goal,
straightest path (1.016), and a velocity command the robot still substantially
executes. Dropping to 0.3 is the only setting that actually costs anything
(+4.3 s), because it is below what the walker can do.

Note what is *not* in this table: clearance is 0.348–0.349 m and the
collision-free rate is 100 % in every row. Speed does not trade against safety
here — for the same reason nothing else does (Q-D, Q-G).

### Q-C — Does it navigate?  → `figures/qc_scenarios.png`, `figures/qc_trajectories.png`

Six scenarios × 10 seeds against an empty-world control.

| scenario | n | collision-free | fell | min clearance [m] | t_goal [s] | path ratio | ZMP RMSE [m] |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| empty (control) | 3 | 100 % | 0 | – | 8.95 | 0.97 | 0.123 |
| head_on | 10 | 100 % | 0 | 0.348 | 9.10 | 0.98 | 0.113 |
| crossing | 10 | 100 % | 0 | 0.349 | 10.42 | 1.03 | 0.113 |
| corridor | 10 | 100 % | 0 | 0.348 | 11.18 | 1.04 | 0.110 |
| circle | 10 | 100 % | 0 | 0.349 | 12.40 | 1.06 | 0.113 |
| random (8 peds) | 10 | 100 % | 0 | 0.348 | 10.12 | 1.05 | 0.114 |

**All stated success criteria are met**: ≥5 moving pedestrians (`random`,
`corridor`, `circle`), zero collisions in 100 % of runs (bar: 80 %), and — from
Q-A — planning well inside real time.

Two things in this table matter more than the success rate.

First, **clearance is pinned at 0.348–0.349 m in every scenario**, identical to
three decimals. That is not a coincidence, it is a constant: ORCA plans with an
inflated robot radius (0.35 physical + 0.2 safety margin) against pedestrians
carrying half the 0.3 m personal-space buffer, so the separation it converges to
is set by those constants and not by the encounter. Q-D varies them to confirm it.

Second, **the path ratio never exceeds 1.06** — the robot walks almost straight
through every crowd (see `figures/qc_trajectories.png`; the visible waviness is
the gait's lateral sway, not avoidance). A robot that never has to deviate and
never collides is either very good or never actually challenged. That is what
Q-G asks.

### Q-D — Who yields?  → `figures/qd_responsibility.png`

`robot_responsibility` ∈ {0.0, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0} × `safety_margin` ∈ {0.1, 0.2, 0.4}.

Responsibility 0.0 means the robot assumes pedestrians will do all the avoiding;
1.0 means it yields entirely. The team currently ships 0.2 — i.e. the robot
mostly expects to be avoided. Whether that is justified is exactly the question.

`[TBD table_qd_responsibility.csv]` — partial (64 of 120):

| responsibility | margin | collision-free | min clearance [m] | t_goal [s] | path ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 0.1 | 90 % | 0.348 | 16.0 | 1.45 |
| 0.0 | 0.2 | 90 % | 0.348 | 16.0 | 1.41 |
| 0.0 | 0.4 | 100 % | 0.348 | 15.0 | 1.19 |
| 0.2 | 0.1 | 100 % | 0.348 | 9.9 | 1.04 |
| 0.2 | 0.2 | 100 % | 0.348 | 10.1 | 1.05 |
| 0.2 | 0.4 | 100 % | 0.348 | 12.4 | 1.17 |
| 0.5 | 0.1 | 100 % | 0.349 | 9.4 | 1.01 |

**The headline is the column that does not move.** Minimum clearance is 0.348 m
in every cell — the robot's safety margin can be 0.1 m or 0.4 m, its
responsibility 0.0 or 0.5, and the achieved clearance does not shift by a
millimetre.

That number is not the robot's. The pedestrians treat the robot as an ORCA
neighbour of radius 0.35 m and each side carries half of the 0.3 m
personal-space buffer, so the crowd converges to 0.35 + 0.15 + 0.3 + 0.15 =
0.95 m of centre separation — a surface gap of 0.95 − 0.3 − 0.3 = **0.35 m**.
The measured value is that constant. **The crowd is setting the clearance, and
the robot's entire safety configuration is inert.**

What the robot's responsibility *does* buy is efficiency, and the effect is
large: at 0.0 the robot needs 15–16 s and walks 1.4× the straight-line distance
(it is shoved around and has to recover); at 0.2–0.5 it needs ~9–10 s at ≈1.0×.
So the shipped 0.2 is a reasonable choice — but for goal efficiency, not for
safety, which is not what the parameter's name suggests.

This is the strongest possible motivation for Q-G: a safety parameter that
changes nothing about safety is what you would expect if the robot were not the
thing doing the avoiding.

### Q-E — Density limit  → `figures/qe_density.png`

`n_peds` ∈ {0, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 24} in the random scenario, 10 seeds each.

| pedestrians | collision-free | falls | clearance [m] | t_goal [s] | path ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 100 % | 0 | – | 8.95 | 0.97 |
| 2 | 100 % | 0 | 0.349 | 9.33 | 0.99 |
| 4 | 100 % | 0 | 0.349 | 9.73 | 1.00 |
| 8 | 100 % | 0 | 0.348 | 10.12 | 1.05 |
| 12 | 100 % | 0 | 0.347 | 10.66 | 1.04 |
| 16 | 100 % | 0 | 0.348 | 10.88 | 1.05 |
| 24 | 100 % | 0 | 0.348 | 11.64 | 1.09 |

**There is no breaking point in the tested range.** Tripling the crowd from 8 to
24 costs 1.5 s and 4 % of path length; clearance and collision-free rate do not
budge. The slide title "where it breaks" has no answer, and that non-answer is
informative: a planner that genuinely had to solve a denser problem would show
*something* — longer detours, closer calls, occasional failures. Nothing degrades
because, as Q-G establishes, the robot is not the one solving it.

### Q-F — Cost of perception  → `figures/qf_perception.png`

Ground truth vs onboard depth camera, paired by (scenario, seed).

**Perception does not lose pedestrians. It inflates them.** (60 runs, 30 pairs)

| scenario | mode | collision-free | t_goal [s] | clearance [m] | tracks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| head_on | ground truth | 100 % | 9.10 | 0.348 | 2.0 / 2 |
| head_on | depth camera | 80 % | 39.38 | 0.349 | 2.0 / 2 |
| crossing | ground truth | 100 % | 10.42 | 0.349 | 4.0 / 4 |
| crossing | depth camera | **10 %** | 35.82 | 0.349 | 4.0 / 4 |
| random | ground truth | 100 % | 10.12 | 0.348 | 8.0 / 8 |
| random | depth camera | **10 %** | 30.29 | 0.348 | 8.0 / 8 |

Perceived radius against a true 0.30 m: **3.14 m median / 8.38 m peak** in
head_on, **4.27 m / 9.45 m** in crossing — 10–30× too large, and worse with
density.

Two numbers carry the result. Collision-free success collapses from 100 % to
10 % and time-to-goal roughly quadruples. Clearance does not move at all:
**Wilcoxon signed-rank over the 30 pairs gives p = 0.76** — no detectable
difference. Perception costs the robot its *goal*, never its *safety*.

Track *count* is correct throughout (2.0/2, 4.0/4, 12.0/12) — the camera sees
everybody. What is wrong is the radius: a true 0.30 m pedestrian is carried as a
3–9 m disk, 10–30× too large, and the inflation grows with crowd density.

The mechanism is in `talos.py`'s radius estimator. It is written to never
under-estimate, on the reasoning that an under-estimate is unsafe for ORCA — so
both of its guards take a maximum against the previous value:

```python
if all(clip[name]):
    radius = max(radius, prev['r'])          # hold outright
else:
    radius = max(radius, 0.99 * prev['r'])   # shrink at most 1% per frame
```

There is no upper bound. A single frame that over-estimates — two pedestrians
merging into one blob, a depth outlier — latches, and the 1 %-per-frame release
is far too slow to recover before the next over-estimate re-inflates it. The
estimate ratchets monotonically upward for the whole run.

Downstream, ORCA sees a neighbour disk that overlaps the robot, drops into its
"already colliding" branch, and commands a full-speed escape: `|v_cmd|` sits at
the 1.5 m/s cap for 97 % of the run, and the robot flees a crowd that is not
where it thinks it is. In head-on there is enough free space to still get around
and 8/10 runs finish; at crossing density none do.

Note what perception does **not** cost: true clearance is 0.348–0.350 m in every
row, the same constant as everywhere else. The robot's beliefs got much worse and
its safety did not change at all — because, as Q-D and Q-G show, its safety was
never the robot's doing.

**Recommended fix** (one line, not applied here — it is the perception owner's
call): clamp the estimate to a plausible pedestrian size, e.g.
`radius = min(radius, 0.6)`. The "never under-estimate" instinct is right; it
just needs a ceiling.

### Q-G — Whose avoidance is it?  → `figures/qg_cooperation.png`

The attribution experiment, added because Q-C was too clean.

Cooperative crowd (shipped default) vs a crowd blind to the robot, paired by
(scenario, density, seed), at four conditions: head-on ×2, crossing ×4,
random ×8, random ×16.

If the collision-free rate and clearance hold up with a blind crowd, the robot's
ORCA layer is genuinely doing the avoidance and every other result stands as
stated. If they collapse, then the honest headline for the whole project is
"the robot walks through a crowd that avoids it", and the Q-C numbers describe
the pedestrians' ORCA rather than the robot's.

**They collapse.** (79 runs, 39 paired)

| condition | crowd | collision-free | clearance [m] | t_goal [s] | path ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| head_on ×2 | cooperative | 100 % | +0.348 | 9.10 | 0.98 |
| head_on ×2 | **blind** | **0 %** | **−0.544** | 15.36 | 1.47 |
| crossing ×4 | cooperative | 100 % | +0.349 | 10.42 | 1.03 |
| crossing ×4 | **blind** | **0 %** | **−0.524** | 20.71 | 1.82 |
| random ×8 | cooperative | 100 % | +0.348 | 10.12 | 1.05 |
| random ×8 | **blind** | **10 %** | **−0.487** | 13.08 | 1.26 |
| random ×16 | cooperative | 100 % | +0.348 | 10.88 | 1.05 |
| random ×16 | **blind** | **0 %** | **−0.558** | 17.84 | 2.14 |

Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank on clearance: **W = 0, p = 3.6 × 10⁻¹²** over 39
pairs. W = 0 means every single pair moved the same way — there is no seed, no
scenario, no density at which removing the crowd's cooperation did not destroy
the result.

A clearance of −0.52 m against radii summing to 0.6 m puts the disks nearly
concentric: the robot walks *through* the pedestrian. Nothing stops it, because
the pedestrians are ghosts (`contact=False`), and the robot never fell in any
blind run — it simply passed through people.

**But the robot is not doing nothing.** Path ratio rises from ≈1.0 to 1.26–2.14
and time-to-goal roughly doubles: the ORCA layer is plainly active and steering
hard. It is just far too slow to matter. Q-B measured why — the robot walks at
0.4 m/s and executes a command 1.57 s (two steps) after ORCA issues it, while
pedestrians close at up to 1.3 m/s. A pedestrian covers ~2 m inside the robot's
reaction lag. **The avoidance is not under-tuned, it is out-run.**

This single experiment reinterprets every other result:

* Q-C's 100 % across six scenarios — the crowd's doing.
* Q-D's clearance constant at 0.348 m under every setting — it was never the
  robot's parameter to move.
* Q-E's absence of any density limit — density cannot challenge a robot that is
  being avoided.
* Q-F's finding that wrecking perception costs the goal but not safety (p = 0.76)
  — safety was never coming from the robot's beliefs.

Each of those was individually puzzling. One fact explains all four.

---

## 4. The story in one paragraph

The system meets every success criterion it was given: 100 % collision-free
navigation across six scenarios and every crowd density up to 24 pedestrians,
with planning far inside real time. It meets them because the pedestrians avoid
the robot. Remove the crowd's cooperation and the collision-free rate goes to
0 % (p = 3.6 × 10⁻¹²) — not because the robot's ORCA layer is idle (it detours
up to 2.1× further) but because it is out-run: it walks at 0.4 m/s and executes
each command two steps (1.57 s) late, while pedestrians close at 1.3 m/s. That
one fact explains four otherwise puzzling results: why clearance is pinned at
0.348 m under every parameter setting, why crowd density never degrades
anything, why breaking perception costs the goal but provably not safety
(p = 0.76), and why the safety margin is inert.

## 5. What we would change

1. **The bridge is too slow for the crowd's speed.** Either slow the crowd to a
   speed the walker can respond to, or shorten the reaction path — react at the
   MPC rate rather than once per 0.8 s step, and shrink the one-step commitment.
   This is the real research problem the project uncovered.
2. **Set `desired_speed ≈ 0.6 m/s`** (Q-B2). Free, and makes the velocity command
   mean something.
3. **Bound the perceived radius** (Q-F). One clamp turns a 10–30× over-estimate
   into a working perception path.
4. **Give pedestrians contact** (`contact=True`) so a collision has a physical
   consequence and the balance controller is actually tested by one.

### Honest limitations to state on slide 13

- Pedestrians are **ghosts** (`contact=False`) — a collision is measured
  geometrically, never physically. In the Q-G blind runs the robot passes
  *through* people without ever losing balance. Collision counts are a planning
  metric, not a safety-of-contact one, and the blind condition would look worse
  still with contact enabled.
- In the default configuration pedestrians avoid the robot **reciprocally using
  ground truth**, even when the robot itself only has camera data — a crowd both
  more cooperative and better informed than any real one. Q-G quantifies exactly
  how much that flatters the results: all of it.
- The crowd model is kinematic disks, not gait-driven humans.
- One robot, one goal, one map; no static obstacles.
- The Q-G blind condition is the opposite extreme: real pedestrians do look
  where they are going. The truth is between the two, and the project has only
  measured the endpoints.
- Timings come from `qa_solo`, run one at a time on an idle machine. The sweep's
  own timings (10 concurrent sims) are ~2× inflated and are not quoted.
